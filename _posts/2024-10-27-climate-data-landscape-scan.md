### Problem

The federal government lacks a coherent environmental data framework—a shared understanding of what climate and ecological data exists, who needs it, and how it connects environmental conditions to the people and places affected.

This isn't an abstract gap. Climate and ecological data is fragmented across agencies with no shared standard, which means communities facing wildfire risk, flood exposure, and climate migration can't be adequately supported. The infrastructure that would link environmental exposure to housing, income, and mobility doesn't cohere — and the populations most exposed are the ones least served by that fragmentation.

This is a problem that lives at the intersection of two systems: the organizational one (divisions, agencies, civic infrastructure) and the cultural one (who is exposed, whose resilience gets measured, whose voice shapes the standard).

---

### Solution

Rather than arriving with a product, I led with learning—designing the work to understand the system before designing for it.

I ran a structured landscape scan and stakeholder consultation: interviewing environmental economists, senior data scientists, and interagency leads across Census's Social, Economic and Housing Statistics Division, the Environmental Impacts Frame initiative, and the interagency natural capital accounting effort.

The research asked the questions that come before a solution — Is there genuine need for a new environmental data product? Who would use it? What data already exists (Census microdata, NOAA, FEMA flood records, remote sensing, tree canopy, vegetation classification), and where are the important gaps? — while mapping the policy systems already in motion (SEEA standards, natural capital satellite accounts, community resilience estimates).

The proof of concept here wasn't a pipeline; it was a stakeholder map and a set of research questions built to facilitate the conversation across the divide, held lightly enough for others to reshape.

---

### Impact

This groundwork makes it possible for federal agencies to eventually understand environmental inequality across time and place — who is exposed to air pollution, flood risk, or wildfire, and how those exposures map onto where and how people live.

The deliverable was designed to be transferable: the stakeholder map and research questions are built to be picked up by whoever secures funding or an institutional home for the next phase, so the value outlives any single fellowship.

The most important finding was institutional — the work that matters most (environment → population health, ecological accounting → economic policy) sits exactly at the intersection where organizational fragmentation makes it hardest to move. Naming that constraint is itself the contribution: the next phase needs dedicated funding, an identified agency owner, together with collaboration with atmospheric scientists, demographers, and policy economists that no single researcher can substitute for.
